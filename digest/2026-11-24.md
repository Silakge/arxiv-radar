## 1. [Beyond Accuracy: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Hypothesis-Driven Experiment Planning in LLM Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29467v1)

`score 100` · cs.LG · 2026-11-22

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Ryo Kuroki, Amin Mansouri, Philippe Schwaller · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29467v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Evaluating large language model (LLM) agents on scientific tasks typically reduces to final-task accuracy, which obscures where reasoning succeeds or breaks down. We introduce a benchmark and diagnostic evaluation framework for hypothesis-driven adaptive experimental planning in closed-world system identification, instantiated in kinetic mechanism identification. The framework goes beyond final accuracy in two ways. First, we evaluate exploration behavior by quantifying the extent to which agents exhibit space-filling or corner-seeking patterns in the experimental design space. Second, we decompose the reasoning process into four interpretable steps: belief update, hypothesis discrimination, discriminative experiment design, and predictive reasoning under interventions, each scored separately on reasoning traces. Across ten LLM agents, we find that stronger agents outperform both adaptive (Bayesian optimization) and non-adaptive (Latin hypercube) baselines and exhibit exploration patterns inconsistent with naive heuristics. Decomposing reasoning reveals that the stronger agents score consistently well across all four steps, whereas weaker agents often succeed at belief update and hypothesis discrimination but are less reliable at translating them into actionable designs and sharp contrastive predictions. Independent expert annotations support predictive reasoning under our stated validation criteria, while evidence for discriminative experiment design is positive but less consistent. The results suggest a gap between recognizing plausible hypotheses and translating them into contrastive predictions, with more tentative evidence for a corresponding experiment-design bottleneck. The benchmark website is available at https://schwallergroup.github.io/beyond-accuracy/.

## 2. [From Rewarding What Models Say to How They Think: Attention Process Reward Model for LLM Reinforcement Learning](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30627v1)

`score 83` · cs.CL · 2026-11-23

**Matched**: reward model

**Authors**: Kairong Han, Nuanqiao Shan, Ziyu Zhao, et al. · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.30627v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an important component in large language models (LLMs). However, existing RL approaches focus on final outputs or textual solution trajectories, leading to coarse-grained rewards and unreliable reasoning. Such paradigms focus mainly on what the model outputs while overlooking how the model internally reasons. Therefore, can we reward how a model thinks internally rather than merely what it says? We first reveal the problem of textual reward aliasing in existing reward modeling approaches. Motivated by this, we propose the Attention Process Reward Model (APRM), which assigns internal rewards based on hidden states and attention maps to capture high-quality reasoning patterns. With only approximately 46M parameters, APRM is trained using ranking and counterfactual objectives, and its reward guides RL optimization while being periodically updated to handle distribution shifts. Integrated with representative RL methods, APRM achieved an average pass@8 gain of 2.88\%. Our results demonstrate the promise of incorporating internal reasoning signals into RL for LLMs.

## 3. [Weight Coding: Evolving Web-Scale Recommenders through Direct Weight Edits with LLM Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29613v1)

`score 75` · cs.IR · 2026-11-22

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Junpeng Hou, Jiaxing Qu, Derek Cheng, et al. · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29613v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Web-scale recommender models evolve primarily through retraining or fine-tuning. We propose \emph{weight coding}, a methodology for model evolution through direct, executable weight edits. A large language model (LLM) agent inspects model structure and parameters, formulates hypotheses, and implements edits using evaluation feedback. The loop progresses from localized changes to targeted searches and composition, allowing edited components to adapt to one another. A cached engine enables rapid forward evaluation without backpropagation. On Pinterest's 147M-parameter Image Search two-tower retriever, weight coding improves held-out recall@10 of production model by up to 15.0 percentage points (+24\% relative) with few edits in about 1/4 GPU hours of a full post-training finetune, while a common launch only improves around 1\%. After reinitializing the entire 13.6M-parameter interaction module randomly while retaining the surrounding trained model, searching 2,448 mixing and normalization parameters yields 9.8 points above production. Offline gains span the full funnel including dense retrieval, generative retrieval and ranking, with up to 1.37B learnable parameters and different edits for each architecture. Steering edits provide explicit control of model performance over segments like freshness and product. Weight coding requires no serving change and online A/B tests increase saves per user from the edited retrieval source by 8.4\% to 10.7\%, aligned with the target business objectives. Our results establish weight coding as a practical and useful tool for model development and and behavioral control in a real web-scale production system, with potential to extend to both pre-training and post-training stages across the funnel.

</details>

## 4. [Attributing Security Outcomes in LLM-Agent Evaluations: The Role of Content-Free State](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30259v1)

`score 50` · cs.CR · 2026-11-23

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Abdelmonaim Bounite, Zangir Iklassov · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.30259v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Security benchmarks for LLM agents record whether each attack succeeded and compare agents, attacks, and defenses by attack success rate (ASR). Such comparisons are valid only if the agent, the attack, and the defense cause each verdict. We show that verdicts also depend on content-free state: details of a run that carry no information about the system under test, such as the Python hash seed, the order of tools or evidence, and the scoring of failed runs. We require that this state influence no verdict (decision noninterference) and treat it as untrusted input, because evaluators and requesters of LLM-based gates can choose it. Our framework, TARE (Testing Attribution of Reported Effects), attributes a reported effect to the agent, attack, or defense only if it exceeds the measured influence of content-free state. On Agent Security Bench (ASB), runs with a fixed hash seed are identical for seven agents from four model families, yet changing the seed changes up to 17% of an agent's verdicts while the ASR moves by at most 5 points. The seed acts through the tool order: sorting the tool list instead of reverse-sorting it raises the ASR by up to 19 points, because ASB's naming convention places attacker tools first. When the same evidence is reordered, six of seven LLM judges change their decision on 17-91% of 98 proposed actions. Across eighteen agent-defense combinations, TARE's worst-case test attributes effects to two of three defenses, one of them in opposite directions on two agents, and a test fixed in advance confirms a 4.8-point decrease from the third; in the attributed decreases, 35-69% of the stopped attacks end without any tool call, which ASB counts as a failed attack. Reproducible verdicts are thus not necessarily attributable, and security evaluations of LLM agents should report the influence of content-free state. We release TARE, the ASB patch, and all verdict tables.

</details>

## 5. [Reading the Tea Leaves, Again: Can Large Language Models Predict Language Model Training Runs?](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30141v1)

`score 25` · cs.CL · 2026-11-23

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Xinran Zhao, Xiang Li, Tong Chen, et al. · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.30141v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

LLM agents now propose research ideas and run the experiments that test them, but they train every run to its end, whereas a researcher reads a run's configuration and the beginning of its curve and stops the runs that show no promise. Reading a run this way takes more than describing or implementing a training method: it takes knowing what a design choice does to a run. We build Tea Leaves to measure this knowledge on 682 real training runs across pretraining, fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning (RL): a forecaster sees the first 20% of a run with its configuration, description, and code, and predicts the rest. Across 11 LLMs and a range of forecasting baselines, the strongest LLMs match or exceed the best curve fitter on smooth loss curves (skill 0.66 against 0.62) and lead on noisy RL reward curves (0.43 against 0.13). What bounds every forecaster is what the log carries: on sparsely logged community runs all methods lose most of their skill, and none reliably flags a failing run in advance. Beyond the curve, information helps and effort does not: related runs in the prompt cut the error on loss curves by 39%, and knowledge of training dynamics lets the strongest LLMs order all 36 pairs of runs that differ by a described code change, which the early curves do not separate, whereas tools add little. This knowledge can be learned from logged runs: a small open model trained on public runs matches the best LLM on loss curves. Applied to the progress curves of research agents, the same reading ranks tasks by their remaining headroom, although for a single agent run it does not improve on the last value. We release the benchmark, the trained readers, and their training data to test and train LLMs' understanding of training and to help research agents decide which runs deserve their compute.

</details>

## 6. [GlobalHRBench: Does Changing the Question Language Change the Legal Answer?](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29852v1)

`score 25` · cs.CL · 2026-11-23

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Yuding Wang, Yuhang Yao · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29852v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Language matters when deploying LLM agents across borders, especially in law, where the governing rules must hold across working languages. Does changing the language of a legal question change answer accuracy? We introduce GlobalHRBench, pairing 240 employment-law problems across eight jurisdictions and eight languages while fixing facts, applicable law and required conclusions. We evaluate nine models on all 1,920 prompts in independent, closed-book, single-answer trials, scoring legal correctness, completeness and answer-language compliance. GPT-6.1 Sol leads at 76.2% strict passes, with a 5.8% relative best-to-worst language decline, compared with 42.2% for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. Kimi K3.1 Pro and Claude Sonnet 5.5 reverse order between English and Chinese. A four-model study on 16 paired families compares closed-book answers with governing evidence, added cross-jurisdiction distractors and removal of a governing passage. Governing evidence corrects legal errors and shortens reasoning on recovered answers, yet recognizing an evidence gap does not ensure correct application of the rules that remain. These findings motivate multilingual training for accurate, efficient and evidence-supported answers, and deployment evaluation in the user's working language.

## 7. [HyperHarness: Learning to Generate Personalized Agent Harnesses from User Histories](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29062v1)

`score 25` · cs.CL · 2026-11-21

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Zhaoxuan Tan, Jiachen Zhao, Jingbo Yang, et al. · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29062v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Personalized LLM agents rely on an agent harness, the surrounding memory and control code, to connect user history with downstream decisions. Existing methods typically employ either a single shared memory workflow across all users, which is efficient to deploy but cannot adapt how evidence is retrieved and interpreted across diverse histories, or per-user harness optimization, which customizes behavior for each user but requires costly repeated candidate evaluation at deployment and suffers from sparse, noisy supervision in raw logs. To address this trade-off, we present HyperHarness, a framework that learns a shared history-to-harness generator to synthesize executable, user-specific retrieval and answer-guidance code directly from user histories. Given a user's permitted history, HyperHarness prompts a construction LLM to write personalized retrieval and guidance functions, while downstream task scores across training users supervise iterative refinements to the shared generator without reference harnesses or answering-model updates. At deployment, the frozen generator constructs a harness for each unseen user in a single pass from history alone, eliminating per-user task-scored search. Across three benchmarks (PersonaMemv3, Mem-PAL, and MemoryCD) and three answering models (Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.8-27B, and GPT-5.6-Luna), HyperHarness achieves the highest aggregate score in seven of nine settings and ranks second in the remaining two, improving over the strongest baselines by up to 5.95 points while cutting matched answer-time tokens by 90.32% over long-context prompting. Analyses show that optimization tailors context allocation to each executor, transfers to unseen user clusters, and amortizes adaptation costs.

## 8. [Mind the Trap As LLMs Guess the Data Model: Grain-Aware Guards Against Fan-out in Text-to-SQL and Its Benchmarks](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29009v1)

`score 25` · cs.DB · 2026-11-21

**Matched**: LLM agents

**Authors**: Ahmed M. Mahran · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29009v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

Text-to-SQL systems built on large language models (LLMs) often aggregate over one-to-many and many-to-many joins, a classic trap: when the joined rows repeat an entity, a SUM or COUNT counts it more than once, yet the query runs and returns a plausible number. We characterize these fan-out errors by one condition on the relation an aggregate reads, which depends on two facts a schema does not show: each measure's grain, and how many rows a join on each key repeats. An audit that tests the condition on the data finds distorted gold answers for 4.2% of the dev questions of BIRD, a widely used benchmark, and BIRD's later, independent revision changed 44% of the answers the audit flags as definite fan-out, against 16% of unflagged ones. We release BIRD-FanOut, 486 BIRD questions with reviewed gold, and GrainTraps, synthetic questions that set this trap and related ones. Across eight LLM agents, functional dependencies (FDs) computed from the data help no more than per-column statistics on GrainTraps, but grain-aware FDs, which also state each measure's grain and the repetition per key, cut fan-out errors on BIRD from 18.4% of runs to about 13%, also on held-out questions and without LLM judges. Accuracy rises less, because most remaining errors are not fan-out and BIRD's hints often prescribe the over-count. Text-to-SQL models should be given the grain of the data, and benchmark gold should be audited on the data. Code, data and benchmarks are available at https://github.com/ahmed-mahran/misqal

## 9. [Sparse Hodge Preference Optimization: Fine-Tuning Language Models toward Alignment beyond Transitive Preferences](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30135v1)

`score 17` · stat.ML · 2026-11-23

**Matched**: reward model

**Authors**: Yiming Liu, Jianwei Ma, Zhongwei Xing, Tonghui Yu · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.30135v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

The growing adoption of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which leverages pairwise preference data, reflects its great success in fine-tuning large language models to align with human expectations. It directly optimizes the policy from human preferences without explicit reward modeling or the complexities of reinforcement learning-based approaches. However, the classic DPO relies on the Bradley--Terry sampling model and cannot effectively capture intransitivity or cyclic structures in aggregated human preference data. In this article, we propose Sparse Hodge Preference Optimization (SHPO), a preference optimization algorithm that augments DPO with a sparse antisymmetric curl residual motivated by the combinatorial Hodge decomposition. We prove that SHPO strictly enlarges DPO's hypothesis class on comparison graphs containing at least one triangle, under some mild regularity conditions on the response representations. We further establish a theoretical guarantee for $\ell_1$-regularized estimation of the curl coefficients under fixed policy and representations. Qualitative studies on three preference datasets under the same QLoRA budget, SHPO-aligned LLM achieves satisfactory performances in preference accuracy, cyclic structure recovery, and GPT-4o-judged generation quality.

## 10. [From Vanishing Gradients to Recursive Self-Improvement: Evaluating the Most Influential Papers in Large Language Model Evolution (1997-2026)](http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29303v1)

`score 17` · cs.CL · 2026-11-22

**Matched**: RLHF

**Authors**: Saadh Jawwadh · [pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29303v1)

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>

The rapid proliferation of Large Language Model (LLM) research makes it increasingly difficult to distinguish incremental refinements from true paradigm shifts. This review evaluates 22 of the most influential publications spanning nearly three decades (1997-2026), organized through a causal, chronologically anchored taxonomy centered on the author's birth year (1999). Beginning "before 1999" with how recurrent neural networks (RNNs) forgot earlier context (much like forgetting Chapter 1 by the time a reader reaches Chapter 9), we trace how Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks introduced a "bouncer" mechanism of forget, input, and output gates to selectively preserve long-term memory. We then examine how Word2Vec turned language into vector mathematics (king - male + female ~ queen), why sequential RNNs hit a parallelization wall, and how the Transformer's all-to-all attention weights launched the modern LLM era alongside a quadratic bottleneck. Next, we analyze why scaling parameters alone leaves capacity under-trained unless data scales equally (Chinchilla), how Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) left alignment superficial, and how that gap compounded into adversarial jailbreaks and single-direction refusal subspaces (J-space), where instructing a model not to think of a restricted concept surfaces it much like the human "red ball" paradox. Finally, we synthesize the current state of AI (2024-2026), encompassing recursive model collapse, autonomous reasoning-model jailbreak agents, machine-speed agentic intrusions, and closed-loop recursive self-improvement (AIDE^2).

</details>
